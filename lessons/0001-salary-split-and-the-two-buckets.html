The Salary Split: Your Piggy Bank vs The Pension Pool

Every month, your company deducts money from your salary for "PF", and adds an equal match. Most salaried professionals assume this entire 24% is compounding in their personal bank account. It is not.

The Layman Analogy: Two Different Buckets

Imagine you and your employer walk to a table with cash every month. The law says 12% of your Basic Pay must come out of your salary, and your employer must match another 12%.

Bucket 1: EPF (Your Personal Lockbox)

All 100% of your employee deduction goes straight into this lockbox.
Only 3.67% of your employer's match goes here.

How it works: This is your actual private money. The Government adds guaranteed compound interest (~8.15%–8.25% per year). What you put in plus interest is 100% yours upon retirement or exit.

Bucket 2: EPS (The Shared Village Pension Pool)

The bulk of your employer's match—8.33% (capped at statutory wage ceiling, max ₹1,250/mo on ₹15k basic)—is diverted away from your personal savings into a national pension pool.

How it works: You never get this capital back with interest. It goes into a communal pool. In exchange, the government promises you a small monthly pension (formula-based, usually ₹1,000 to ₹3,500/month) after age 58, provided you worked at least 10 years.

Why This Matters for Your Wealth

Because 8.33% of basic goes to EPS rather than your compounding account, high earners who don't need a ₹2,000 monthly pension lose compounding power on that employer match.

Key Rule: If your basic salary at your very first EPF job was over ₹15,000/month, you are an "excluded employee" from EPS—meaning your employer can put the full 12% into your personal EPF lockbox instead of wasting it on EPS!
